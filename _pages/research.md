Dissertation
------

**Decentralized Yet Determined: Sustaining Transnational "Comfort Women" Reparation Lawsuits**

Why do survivors of historical injustice and their supporters keep turning to courts after decades of legal defeat? My dissertation answers this question through the transnational reparations litigation brought by survivors of Japanese military sexual slavery in Korean, Japanese, and U.S. courts since the early 1990s. Where existing accounts treat this litigation as a story of failure, or as a by-product of state-to-state diplomacy, I show that it has been sustained by a decentralized but determined support structure of lawyers, activists, scholars, and survivors operating across borders. Drawing on the literature on transnational advocacy networks and legal mobilization, I argue that lawsuits in third-country courts, above all in the United States, became a means for survivors to pursue recognition and redress when state-centered mechanisms failed them.

The project rests on more than two years of multi-sited fieldwork in South Korea (2022–2024), including participant observation in the Wednesday Demonstration community, interviews with lawyers, activists, and researchers, and archival research on court records and movement documents. It contributes to debates on legal mobilization, victim agency, postcolonial politics, and the meaning of transitional justice for conflict-related sexual violence.

An article drawn from the dissertation, *Decentralized Yet Determined: Sustaining Transnational "Comfort Women" Reparation Lawsuits*, is currently under review.

Related work
------

My earlier work re-examined Allied wartime interrogation records to reassess the systemic character of Japanese military sexual slavery (*Society and History*, 2017), and a recent chapter reflects on the expansion of "wartime sexual violence" as a category and the voices it omits (Northeast Asian History Foundation, 2026). I have also reviewed scholarship on the prosecution of sexual and gender-based crimes at the International Criminal Court for *Governance*.

Methods and languages
------

Qualitative and ethnographic methods, interviews, and archival research; NVivo and MAXQDA; R and Python. I work in Korean (native), English (fluent), and Japanese (advanced).
